## Abstract

We investigate whether gravity and inertia can be derived, rather than
postulated, from a single local rewriting rule acting on a relational
substrate — in the spirit of the Wolfram Physics Project's hypergraph
models. The motivating observation is elementary: a free-falling observer
and a deep-space observer both measure zero proper acceleration, while a
stationary observer on a planetary surface measures a nonzero value. We
take this as evidence that gravity and inertia may be two expressions of
one underlying phenomenon, and we ask whether a suitable relational
substrate can reproduce that behavior from first principles, with **mass**
identified as the self-reuse of local update activity and **inertia** as
the cost, in rewrite events, of redirecting it.

This report documents what has actually been established. We find that a
natural and well-studied candidate rule (the canonical Wolfram Physics
Project example) supports well-defined, translating, phase-bearing
recurrent structures, but that these structures are **fragile**: ordinary
background activity damages them, and — across every construction tested —
they essentially never self-repair. We give four structural results
explaining why (a relation-count conservation law, a causal-directionality
constraint, a local-adjacency requirement, and an absorption theorem for
fully severed structures), report five independent engineering attempts to
escape these constraints, and describe a breakthrough using richer
(ternary) relations that solves the local-repair problem completely but
introduces a new, unsolved problem with the emergent background geometry.
We report an exact empirical criterion (PRO) that predicted repair
outcomes perfectly across 604 historical test cases, and its subsequent
falsification — in a pre-registered, out-of-sample test on genuinely
ternary damage — which revealed a more complete three-part hierarchy
(access, relation-floor capacity, and dynamical role transport) governing
repair, now reformulated as reachability into a basin of attraction. We
reframe the particle candidate itself as a closed orbit of the vacuum's
own generative rule rather than a separate object embedded in it, identify
the one native structure with unconditional self-maintenance as a
candidate for the *massless* rather than massive case, and state a
concrete measurement program — rest recurrence energy, a dispersion
relation defining inertial mass, and vacuum backreaction — against which
any future surviving structure, massive or massless, can be tested. An
exhaustive search of 77,314 candidate rules subsequently produced 80 with
a closed, full-constituent-turnover particle class; the strongest, rule
55744, achieves exact universal closure under its complete legal action
grammar and 96.7–96.9% natural-vacuum survival, stable from 2,000 to
80,000 events — for the first time in this investigation, a candidate
robust enough for the measurement program to be run on directly, though
its emergent geometry (local dimension ≈4.6) does not yet resemble
physical space. We state plainly where this leaves the original question:
**no measurement of mass, inertia, or gravitational coupling has yet been
made.** Everything reported here is necessary groundwork toward being able
to make one.

## 1. Motivation

Consider three observers: one in free fall near a planet, one floating in
deep space far from any mass, and one standing on the planet's surface.
The first two both read zero on an accelerometer. The third reads a
nonzero value (locally, about 9.8 m/s² on Earth). Ordinary presentation
treats "gravity" (why the third observer feels a force) and "inertia"
(why *any* observer resists a change in motion) as related by an
empirical coincidence — the equality of gravitational and inertial mass.
The working hypothesis behind this investigation is that this coincidence
is not a coincidence: that both phenomena are the response of one
underlying substrate to the presence of persistent structure, and that a
sufficiently minimal computational substrate might reproduce both from a
single rule.

We adopt the framework of the Wolfram Physics Project: a substrate with no
primitive space, time, or matter — only a set of relations, rewritten
according to one fixed local rule, applied wherever it matches. **Mass**
is provisionally identified with the degree to which a local region's
update activity re-uses its own material rather than dispersing into
fresh material. **Inertia** is the additional rewrite-cost of redirecting
such a region once it is in motion. Under this identification, a
persistent, self-reusing, translating structure is the minimal object on
which either quantity could be measured — so the first task is to find
one, and confirm it survives.

## 2. The substrate

### 2.1 The rule

The canonical rewriting rule used throughout this investigation, adopted
directly from the Wolfram Physics Project's own worked example, is:

$$
(x,y),\,(x,z) \;\longrightarrow\; (x,z),\,(x,w),\,(y,w),\,(z,w)
$$

Here each relation $(a,b)$ is read as a directed link, "$a$ relates to
$b$." The rule matches any two relations sharing a common source $x$, and
replaces them with four relations involving a single freshly created node
$w$. Applied repeatedly, wherever it matches, with no global scheduler or
privileged order, this rule is the entire dynamical content of the model.

Right: its replacement, introducing one new node w.*

### 2.2 The vacuum

Grown from a small seed (three self-loops on a single node), this rule
produces a large, connected, sprawling structure whose large-scale
geometry can be measured empirically: count how the number of reachable
nodes grows as a function of graph distance from a point, the same way

## 3. Candidate matter: persistent recurrent structures

A **particle candidate** is a small region of the network whose relations
cycle through a fixed sequence of shapes, indefinitely, as the rule fires
on it — the discrete analogue of a limit cycle. The primary example used
throughout this investigation is a 4-node structure with period 2,
alternating between a complete bipartite ("biclique") shape and a
"diamond" shape:

$$
M_0 = \{0\!\to\!2,\ 0\!\to\!3,\ 1\!\to\!2,\ 1\!\to\!3\}
\qquad
M_1 = \{0\!\to\!1,\ 0\!\to\!2,\ 1\!\to\!3,\ 2\!\to\!3\}
$$

![The particle loop](figures/fig2_particle.png)

*Figure 3. The M0/M1 recurrence: both legal moves from M0 lead to M1, and
the move from M1 leads back to M0. Undisturbed, this cycle is exact and
indefinite.*

A systematic census later confirmed this is one of exactly 16 such
recurrent structures (71 total phase states) through support size $m\le6$,
extended to 428 phase states across 67 cycles through $m\le7$.

### 3.1 Particles as closed orbits of the vacuum's own rule

It is tempting to describe a particle candidate as a separate mechanism
"riding on top of" the vacuum — a second process layered onto the first.
This framing should be resisted. In every architecture that has actually
worked in this investigation (the canonical rule, C4-T2, C4-T3), there is
exactly one rule. Vacuum and particle are not two different generative
processes; they are two different *behaviors* the same firing events
produce depending on the local topology they land on. Most configurations,
fired on repeatedly, disperse outward into ever-fresh material — that
behavior is what we call vacuum. A rare, special configuration, fired on
repeatedly, returns to itself — that behavior is what we call a particle.
The particle does not borrow the vacuum's machinery; it *is* the vacuum's
machinery, closed into a loop rather than left open. Indirect support for
this comes from Section 6: the one architecture tested that deliberately
separated particle-maintenance machinery from ordinary vacuum relations
(the two-species rule) was among the least successful — it required
gating, it leaked into the vacuum regardless, and merely having the extra
machinery available destabilized the clean cycle even absent damage.

The most precise available terminology, borrowed from dynamical systems
theory, is **attractor** or **limit cycle**: a single dynamical law can
produce both generic diverging trajectories and rare, stable, self-
returning orbits, without needing two different laws to do so. The closer
physical analogy is a **soliton**: not a distinct substance governed by a
separate equation, but a self-reinforcing solution of the same governing
equation as the ordinary waves around it, kept from dispersing by the
medium's own nonlinearity. **Vortex** is apt for the same reason — a
vortex in a fluid is not a different material or a different physics from
the surrounding fluid, only the same fluid organized into a self-sustaining
circulation. A particle, in this framework, is a closed orbit of the
vacuum's own rule, not a passenger on it.

This framing also clarifies what "time" means here, without needing to
assume it. Every notion of generation or age used throughout Sections 5–8
is precisely the depth of a node in the causal dependency graph — which
event had to occur before which other event could occur. Section 5.2's
directionality result is a direct statement about this same causal partial
order: time, in this model, is not a background parameter but the
accumulation of causal steps, exactly as many of the events analyzed in
this report already treat it.

**What remains genuinely open** is *nucleation*: what triggers a particle
candidate to begin existing in the first place, as opposed to the vacuum's
ordinary diverging behavior. Section 9.1 already reports that this has
been tested directly exactly once (the C4-T3 spontaneous-generation check)
and failed completely — 0 of 539,622 candidate sites. Tracing the
provenance of every particle-triggering event backward through the causal
graph would, in this model, terminate at the same initial seed that every
vacuum node traces back to — a coherent inference within the model's own
logic, and a suggestive echo of every particle's energy ultimately tracing
to a single originating event, but it is an extrapolation, not a result,
and it presently rests on a premise (that particles are nucleatable at
all) that has not yet been demonstrated. This question is set aside for
the remainder of this report, which focuses on the demonstrably prior
question of survival.

**This reframing was reached independently, from a different direction,
in a companion analysis of Stages S–U** — working forward from Stage U's
role-transport finding rather than from the vortex/soliton analogy used
above, and arriving at the same conclusion: a viable particle is a
recurrent defect *of* the vacuum's dynamics, not a separate object moving
through it. That companion analysis adds a further, useful sharpening of
what "closed orbit" should mean at the level of individual vertices, not
just at the level of the rule: particle identity should reside in the
*recurrence of relations*, not in the persistence of any particular node.
A structure with the strongest possible version of this property would
satisfy $V_{\text{original}} \cap V_{\text{current}} = \varnothing$ after
one full recurrence period — every original vertex retired and replaced,
with the predictive state nonetheless returning to the same class. Neither
the C2 family (period 2, but two of its four support vertices never turn
over at all) nor the triangle (full turnover, but only after 3 steps, not
within its period-1 cycle) currently satisfies this in its strongest form;
it is best read as a design target for the next construction — a
**full-constituent-turnover requirement** — rather than a property already
demonstrated anywhere in this report.

## 4. The central obstruction

Take a clean instance of $M_0$ and subject it to a single ordinary outside
event of the same kind that occurs constantly throughout the vacuum. Of
the six possible ways such an event can land, two continue the clean
recurrence, two preserve it, and **two destroy a required relation while
retaining an irrelevant one** — an exact 1/3 destructive fraction,
independent of structure size or period.

![The central problem](figures/fig3_damage.png)

*Figure 4. An ordinary background event damages the loop. In every
rigorous test performed in this investigation, the loop's own subsequent
dynamics never recover the lost relation.*

This finding was established with increasing rigor over the course of the
investigation:

- Complete census: all 142 destructive branches were tested in the complete
  fixed-size predictive quotient, and none returned to the original
  recurrence cycle (primary: `CANONICAL_0A4_K_REPORT.md`).
- An exact, non-enumerative proof (Section 5.1) shows this is impossible
  for the entire fixed-support architecture, at any size.
- Real, naturally-evolving vacuum ensembles (8 vacua × 8000 events each)
  measured a natural repair availability of **13.45%** for these
  particles — lower than the 31.42% measured for generic non-recurrent
  structures under identical conditions.
- Direct persistence modeling found 0 of 48 natural carrier instances
  survived to 20 phase advances; the repair rate required for even 50%
  survival to 100 cycles is approximately **97%**. Every measured rate in
  this investigation has fallen well short.

## 5. Four structural explanations

### 5.1 L — the relation-floor conservation law

Define the *relation floor* $\rho(C)$ of a predictive-equivalence class
$C$ as the minimum relation count of any micrograph within it. The
following holds exactly, for every clean and destructive transition, at
any support size — proven by induction, not sampled:

$$
\rho_{\text{next}} \le \rho_{\text{current}}
\qquad\text{(clean move)}
\qquad\qquad
\rho_{\text{damaged}} \le \rho_{\text{current}} - 1
\qquad\text{(destructive move)}
$$

Because a recurrent cycle requires every phase to sit at the same floor,
and the floor can never rise under ordinary dynamics, a damaged structure
can never regain the floor its own cycle requires.

![The conservation law](figures/fig10_L_relationfloor.png)

*Figure 5. Left: the relation floor stays flat under clean evolution and
drops, permanently, after a single destructive event. Right: a concrete
clean transition, showing the relation count preserved.*

### 5.2 Q — causal directionality

Every relation this rule creates points from older, pre-existing material
toward the fresh node created in that same event — never the reverse.

![One-way time](figures/fig11_Q_directionality.png)

*Figure 6. Relations only ever point toward later generations. This makes
any cross-coupling between two separately-evented subsystems (for
instance, a moving "core" and an independent "clock") strictly
one-directional: once consumed, it can never be regenerated. This rules
out an entire class of composite constructions, not merely the specific
instances tested.*

### 5.3 R — the adjacency requirement

Self-maintenance requires the two "frontier" targets of a firing source
to already share a direct relation with each other.

![The adjacency requirement](figures/fig12_R_adjacency.png)

*Figure 7. The 3-node "transitive triangle" has this property natively —
it is the only structure found, through m=7, that does. Every
phase-bearing (period > 1) recurrent structure found lacks it.*

This identifies a genuine trade-off: the one structure with native
self-repair capacity has no internal state to track (period 1); every
structure with an internal clock lacks the pre-wired backup.

### 5.4 S — zero-incidence absorption

If a persistent support vertex loses *every* incident relation as a
result of damage, no future event of this rule family can ever reconnect
it — because every event's output relations are confined to vertices
already part of that event's own match, plus one freshly created vertex,
never a pre-existing, unmatched vertex sitting elsewhere in the graph.
This was proven as a general theorem (for any arity, direction,
multiplicity, or growth law satisfying a natural "relation-generated
matching" assumption) after an initial, broader version of the claim was
shown false by explicit counterexample — the corrected theorem is exact,
but narrower than first proposed: of the 604 historical damage branches
subsequently audited, only those from the ternary construction (Section
7) actually produced a true zero-incidence orphan; the rest left exactly
one surviving relation, a harder case addressed in Section 8. Section
10.2d later identifies this as a corollary of a single more general
theorem, also covering the translation program's no-unseen-frontier
lemma.

## 7. The ternary breakthrough

### 7.1 C4-T2: solving local coverage

The decisive change was moving from binary relations to genuine three-way
relations, $T(x;\{y,z\})$ — "$x$ relates jointly to the unordered pair
$\{y,z\}$" — allowing a single relational object to encode what
previously required two separately-regenerated binary edges to line up
correctly.

$$
T(x;\{y,z\}),\ T(y;\{z,x\})
\;\longrightarrow\;
T(y;\{z,u\}),\ T(z;\{u,y\}),\ T(y;\{v,w\}),\ T(w;\{z,u\})
$$

Across an exhaustive census of 24,408 connected recurrent structures
through $m\le5$ (49,014 phase states), this rule achieved **100% all-phase
frontier coverage** — the first architecture in the investigation to
clear this bar at all, let alone completely.

![The C4-T3 growth rule as hyperedges](figures/fig9_wolfram_C4T3.png)

*Figure 9. The ternary growth rule (Section 7.2), drawn as genuine
hyperedges for the first time — each shaded wedge is one three-way
relation. The pattern visibly rotates one position between BEFORE and
AFTER, which is the shift-register mechanism described algebraically
below.*

### 7.2 C4-T3: solving vacuum extensivity, at a cost

C4-T2's vacuum is exactly relation-count-neutral (2→2 per event, always
+1 vertex), which provably forces the connected fraction of any finite
seed toward zero. A gated growth extension was added — legal only where a
specific "donor" and "marker" motif is present, proven absent from every
particle state — implementing a five-role shift register:

$$
(x,y,z;u,v) \;\longrightarrow\; (y,z,u;v,w)
$$

This produces genuine extensive growth (connected fraction → 0.9999875 at
80,000 events, bounded degree, no dimensional drift), but the resulting
geometry is **exactly one-dimensional** — a strict shift register is
topologically a line, however large it grows. A further, exact result
showed that even with perfect frontier coverage, this construction's
destructive-damage event isolates the fresh lineage node completely
(zero incidence — covered by Section 5.4), giving **0 of 98,538** exact
internal repairs, and that the particle pattern **never spontaneously
appears** in the vacuum's own natural growth (0 of 539,622 induced
subgraphs checked) — because the same gating that protects the particle
from vacuum interference also prevents the vacuum from ever producing it.

### 7.3 Rule 55744: closure achieved, via branching-as-identity

Stage V's diagnosis (Section 8.2) established that closure — not
recurrence, not natural occurrence — was the actual bottleneck, and that
the 493-rule family it searched failed uniformly because branching always
opened at least one escape channel. An exhaustive follow-on search of a
larger, structurally branching eight-output rule family (77,314 candidates)
resolved this. Eighty rules contain a closed, full-constituent-turnover
particle SCC (66 with period 2, 14 with period 3); the selected candidate,
rule 55744, has the internal transition structure

$$
A \to B, \qquad C \to B, \qquad B \to A \ \text{or}\ B \to C,
$$

with **every legal event from every state in this class remaining in the
class** — branching is internal to particle identity rather than a decay
channel, resolving the tension Section 8.2 identified. This is the first
candidate in the investigation to satisfy the universal closure criterion
$\forall a \in A(\Phi): T_a(\Phi) \in \mathcal{B}(\Phi)$ exhaustively rather
than for a single nominal trajectory.

*Figure 4b. Rule 55744's rewrite step, drawn as genuine ternary
hyperedges — verified directly against the source implementation

## 8. Unification: the PRO criterion

The harder open question, after Section 5.4 narrowed the zero-incidence
result, was the regime where exactly one relation survives damage.
Retrospective analysis across 604 branches spanning four architectures
(canonical, V1/V2, two-species, shared-ancestry) found an exact
criterion — **Predictively Required Role Occupancy (PRO)** — with four
conditions:

1. The damage must be a perturbation of the carrier's actual clean
   producer action, not an irrelevant event.
2. The firing source's two frontier targets must already have the direct
   bridge relation R requires.
3. The single surviving relation must occupy the correct (downstream)
   endpoint of that bridge, not the wrong one.
4. The damaged state must still support the carrier's *next* required
   move, not merely the immediate repair.

![The PRO checklist](figures/fig13_T_PRO_flowchart.png)

*Figure 10. All four conditions holding predicts repair with 100%
accuracy; any one failing predicts no repair with 100% accuracy, across
604 tested branches (Fisher's exact test, $p = 5.4\times10^{-60}$).*

This was, at the time, an **exact retrospective empirical equivalence**,
not a proven theorem — the criterion was derived from, and had only been
validated against, a historical corpus that was entirely binary or
binary-plus-maintenance. Section 8.1 reports the pre-registered
out-of-sample test that followed.

### 8.1 PRO is falsified out of sample, and the falsification is informative

A genuinely ternary, single-incidence ($I=1$) damage construction was
built under the C4 architecture, with the PRO classification for every
branch recorded *before* any repair search was run — the discipline
proposed specifically to prevent the criterion's own construction from
contaminating its validation. Across 92 exact branches (two independent
damage mechanisms, "rotating" and "shift"), the result cleanly falsifies
PRO as an arity-independent criterion, in both directions at once:

$$
\boxed{0/8}\ \text{PRO-positive branches repaired (not sufficient)}
\qquad\qquad
\boxed{2}\ \text{PRO-negative branches repaired (not necessary)}
$$

![Stage U falsification](figures/fig14_U_falsification.png)

*Figure 11. Left: the full 2×2 outcome — PRO predicts incorrectly in both
directions. Right: repair outcomes stratified by relation-floor surplus or
deficit ($H_D - H_T$); adequate capacity is necessary but still not
sufficient.*

The mechanism behind each half of the falsification was fully diagnosed,
not left as an anomaly:

**Why the 8 PRO-positive branches fail.** C4's ternary kernel obeys its
own exact relation-count law, directly analogous to Section 5.1:

$$
H' = H - I_x + 2, \qquad I_x \ge 2 \ \Rightarrow\ H' \le H
$$

All 46 "rotating"-damage branches begin one relation short of what the
clean target requires ($H_D = H_T - 1$). Because support hyperedge count
can never increase under ordinary evolution, these branches are exactly
unrepairable regardless of local role occupancy — PRO checked whether the
*correct* relation survived, but never checked whether *enough* relational
capacity survived. In the binary V1/V2 corpus these two properties always
happened to coincide; in the ternary regime they come apart.

**Why 2 PRO-negative branches succeed anyway.** In two branches (both from
the same recurrent SCC), the sole surviving relation occupies the *wrong*
role by PRO's static, single-snapshot test — yet one further legal
support-internal move relabels it into exactly the role the clean
successor needs, reaching the target exactly. PRO's instantaneous check
cannot see this because the relation is transported into the correct role
by *dynamics*, not held in it from the moment of damage.

Stratifying all 92 branches by relation-floor surplus confirms capacity is
necessary but still not the whole story: 0/58 deficit branches repair (as
the exact monotonicity argument requires), 0/28 exactly-adequate branches
repair, and only 2/6 surplus branches repair — the other four fail for
want of an available transport path even with spare capacity in hand.

**Revised hierarchy.** The combined evidence now supports three
independent, ordered requirements rather than one static checklist:

1. **Access** (Stage S) — a fully severed role can never be recovered, at
   any capacity.
2. **Relation-floor capacity** (Stage U) — insufficient total relational
   capacity forbids repair regardless of role occupancy, the ternary
   analogue of Section 5.1.
3. **Dynamical role transport** — sufficient capacity is still not
   sufficient; a valid path must exist that transports the retained
   relation, across zero or more legal support-internal moves, into a role
   the future producer grammar actually requires.

The proposed next criterion — a **Role-Transport / Capacity Criterion**,
defined from the rule's own structure rather than fitted to outcomes, and
checked against three exact benchmark classes established by this stage
(58 capacity-impossible, 28 capacity-adequate-but-unrepaired, 6
capacity-surplus branches, of which exactly 2 repair) — is the current
target for a genuinely arity-independent successor to PRO. One
methodological note for that stage: because the 6-branch benchmark is
already known from Stage U, a fully blind test of the new criterion will
still require a fresh, not-yet-examined branch set, the same discipline
Stage U itself applied.

### 8.2 Toward a basin-of-attraction definition of repair

Stage U's transport counterexamples point to a specific error in how
repair has been defined throughout Sections 4–8: as a property of a
single post-damage snapshot, checked once, immediately. That definition
was always going to miss cases where a relation sits in the wrong role at
the moment of damage but is carried into the right one by an intervening
legal move — exactly the two branches that falsified PRO.

The corrected definition treats repair as reachability into a basin, not
occupancy of a state. For a recurrent predictive class $[\Phi]$, define
its basin $\mathcal{B}(\Phi)$ as the set of states that eventually return
to it under the rule's own dynamics. Damage $D(\Phi)$ is repairable
exactly when

$$
D(\Phi) \in \mathcal{B}(\Phi)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\exists\, n:\ F^n(D(\Phi)) \in [\Phi]_{\text{predictive}}
$$

for the rule's own update map $F$. Under this definition, access (Section
5.4), relation-floor capacity (Section 8.1), and dynamical role transport
(Section 8.1) are not three independent checks to be run in sequence —
they are the three necessary conditions for $\mathcal{B}(\Phi)$ to be
nonempty and reachable at all. Static adjacency (Section 5.3) and PRO's
instantaneous role occupancy (Section 8) should now be read as special-case
proxies for basin membership, correct exactly when the basin happens to
be reached in zero further steps — which is why they worked perfectly on
the historical binary corpus and failed exactly where transport requires
one or more additional steps.

**A necessary refinement, from Stage V.** The definition above is
existential — it asks whether *some* legal path returns damage to the
recurrent class — and is silent on a logically prior question: does the
*clean*, undamaged orbit's own full legal branching remain inside its own
class at all? Testing a branching candidate (C295, the strongest output of
the structured search recommended in Section 11) under its complete legal
action set, not merely the single nominal trajectory that defines the
orbit, found that only 1 of 9 and 1 of 8 legal actions from its two phases
continue the intended recurrence — every other branch is an immediate,
and (by the relation-floor argument of Section 5.1, extended to this rule)
irreversible, escape. Under a uniform local scheduler, expected lifetime
is about 1.13 events, and survival to complete even one full witnessed
cycle is $1/72 \approx 1.4\%$. The correct closure criterion is therefore
the stronger, universal statement

$$
\forall\, a \in A(\Phi):\quad T_a(\Phi) \in \mathcal{B}(\Phi),
$$

every legal action available *from* the orbit itself, not only damage
recovery, must remain in the basin. Existential repairability (C295 had a
substantial one: 40/40 single-relation additions returned; 12/20 deletions
did) is necessary but not sufficient — a candidate can have a real repair
basin and still fail to be a stable particle, if its own undisturbed
branching already leaves that basin almost immediately. Reformulating the
pending Role-Transport / Capacity Criterion to require both existential
repairability *and* universal closure, directly in terms of
$\mathcal{B}(\Phi)$, is a more durable target than continuing to patch
either checklist independently.

## 9. Current state, relative to the original question

### 9.1 A methodological caveat that applies to nearly everything above

Before summarizing what has and hasn't been established, one distinction
needs to be made explicit, because it has not been applied consistently
across this investigation and materially affects how every repair result
above should be read.

![Three experimental postures](figures/fig15_embedding_postures.png)

*Figure 12. Three distinct questions have been asked at different points in
this investigation, under three different experimental setups, and they
are not interchangeable.*

The large majority of results reported above (Sections 5–8, and every
"exact repair census") tested a hand-constructed particle candidate in
total isolation — a small, closed, finite support with no surrounding
vacuum at all. This answers: *if such a structure exists, can it survive
damage?* Only Stage N (and, partially, Stage O) embedded the particle in
genuine, independently-grown vacuum material, and even then as a static
snapshot rather than a live, concurrently-evolving background. A third,
logically prior question — *does this structure ever come to exist in the
first place, from the vacuum's own unprompted dynamics* — has been tested
directly exactly once, in Section 7.2's spontaneous-generation check, with
a clean and total negative: 0 of 539,622 candidate subgraphs. Every
particle instance analyzed anywhere else in this report, in every
architecture, was placed by hand.

This means the survival results in Sections 5–8 should be read as
conditional: *if* a structure like this could come to exist, *then* here
is what would happen to it under damage. Whether it could come to exist
at all — under any architecture tested so far — remains almost entirely
open, and the one direct test of it failed. Closing this gap (a
genuine-embedding, concurrently-growing test of both survival and genesis
together, for whichever architecture eventually clears Section 11's
open items) should be treated as a prerequisite for the investigation's
conclusions to apply to anything other than a hand-placed object.

### 9.2 What is and is not established

**Established:** a rigorous, increasingly precise account of why small
recurrent structures in this substrate are fragile, four structural
reasons why, five independent engineering attempts and their exact
failure modes, a construction that solves local repairability completely
at the cost of an unresolved vacuum-geometry problem, and a sharp,
though not yet general, predictive criterion unifying all of the above.

**Not yet established:** anything resembling mass, inertia, or
gravitational coupling. Every result in this report answers a strictly
prior question — can a candidate structure survive perturbation long
enough to have stable, measurable properties at all? A structure that
falls apart on contact cannot have its mass or inertia meaningfully
measured. The identification of mass with self-reuse and inertia with
redirection-cost (Section 1) remains a working hypothesis, not yet
applied to any surviving object, because no sufficiently robust,
*moving* object has yet been found.

**What would put this investigation back on the direct path:** a
structure that (a) clears the PRO checklist reliably, not merely
sometimes, and (b) is genuinely translating, so that "resistance to
redirection" is a meaningful quantity to measure on it. At that point —
and only at that point — the equivalence-principle question this
investigation exists to answer becomes directly askable: does the
inertial cost of redirecting such a structure scale proportionally with
its measurable effect on the surrounding vacuum's geometry. Section 10
below states this as a concrete, falsifiable measurement program rather
than a general aspiration.

For context: a literature search found no evidence that the primary
Wolfram Physics Project literature has solved this problem either.
Stephen Wolfram's own most recent public writing (February 2026) states
that individual particle masses remain undetermined and dependent on
rule-specific details not yet identified — consistent with, not contrary
to, the difficulty documented here.

  fragments were surgically deleted outright — cut out of the
  structure entirely — and nothing happened. The bond kept behaving
  exactly as if they'd never been removed. Cutting out an even larger
  region around those fragments still changed nothing about the
  bond's future. To make sure this wasn't just because the test was
  too gentle to notice anything, a much cruder cut was also tried —
  removing large stretches of the ordinary surrounding material that
  had nothing to do with formation history at all — and that cut did
  cause a real, delayed disturbance that reached the bond after a
  predictable number of rounds, reproduced exactly at a second,
  independent location. That's exactly the right control: it shows
  the method is sharp enough to catch a real effect when one exists,
  which means its silence on the formation fragments actually means
  something. Together these results draw a clean line between two
  things that easily could have been confused with each other: the
  bond has genuinely let go of its own history, but it hasn't become
  fully self-sufficient — it still depends on ordinary surrounding
  material just to keep existing, which is a different and, in some
  ways, more interesting question to chase next.
- **Stage Y132 then explained that dependency, and resisted a more
  exciting-sounding but wrong explanation along the way.** The delay
  pattern from the previous stage — outer regions being affected
  before inner ones, on a precise, repeatable schedule — looks exactly
  like something traveling inward toward the bond. Careful tracing of
  the specific pieces that actually went missing showed that isn't
  what's happening at all: every single one of them was part of the
  original deletion from the very start, sitting exactly where it had
  always been. Nothing new was created and nothing moved toward the
  bond — the bond itself moved forward and simply arrived at a stretch
  of empty space that had been empty the whole time. That's a much
  more mundane explanation than a propagating disturbance, and it's
  the correct one, confirmed a second way: cutting out only a limited
  patch of surroundings causes a temporary problem that clears up
  completely once the bond travels past the gap and reaches untouched
  material again — without any of the removed material ever coming
  back. Throughout all of this, the bond's actual motion never
  wavered even while its internal bookkeeping was failing, showing
  those two things are more independent of each other than they might
  have seemed. And tracing exactly where the two bond types draw
  their ongoing supply from turned up one more clean difference
  between them: one type pulls everything it needs fresh from the
  surroundings, while the other quietly manufactures part of what it
  needs on its own.
- **Stage Y133 then completed the picture by testing the two sectors
  that hadn't been checked yet, and the two turned out to work in
  genuinely different ways rather than being variations on one
  theme.** Moving at the slower of the two speeds behaves exactly like
  the faster one already studied — the bond keeps drawing fresh
  material from the surroundings as it travels, just encountering the
  need for it later. But a bond that isn't moving at all does
  something different: instead of continually reaching out for new
  material, it reuses the exact same small, fixed pool of material
  over and over, cycle after cycle, never needing anything from
  farther away. Removing material from a wide area around a resting
  bond changes nothing at all; only reaching directly into that small
  reused pool has any effect, and even then the bond's own resting
  behavior carries on unaffected.
- **Stage Y134 then explained something that had been an open loose
  end for many stages — exactly why one of the two bond types briefly
  gets stronger right after speeding up.** Tracking individual pieces
  of material through the moment of a speed change showed the answer
  precisely: right after the switch, the bond first exchanges its old,
  reused resting material for freshly drawn surrounding material, and
  then, one step later, it manufactures one additional piece of
  material on its own and adds that on top. The apparent boost was
  never a coincidence or a side effect — it's the bond doing two
  separate things back to back, now traced exactly. A similar,
  smaller, previously-unremarked blip in the other bond type turned
  out to be the same kind of event: one extra self-made piece that
  appears for exactly one step and then leaves again.
- **Stage Y135 then asked the question this whole environmental thread
  had really been building toward — does any of this material actually
  cause the bond to move the way it does, or does it just quietly
  respond after the fact? — and answered it about as cleanly as a
  question like that can be answered.** Every single test that
  interfered with the supporting material — removing exactly the
  piece a bond was about to use, blocking the self-made piece from
  appearing, even deleting every single supporting piece a resting
  bond had ever relied on, all at once, right before it started to
  move — left the requested motion completely unaffected every time.
  The bond moved exactly as instructed regardless of whether any of
  its supporting material was there or not. That's a clean, honestly
  disappointing answer to the most exciting-sounding possibility this
  whole thread had raised: this environmental material isn't what
  makes the bond resist or accept a change in motion, at least not in
  how the model currently decides when a change happens. What decides
  that is simply told to happen from outside, and none of what's been
  found actually feeds back into that decision. That's a genuinely
  useful thing to know, precisely because it tells you what would
  actually have to change before this line of investigation could
  ever speak to anything resembling inertia: the model would need a
  way of choosing its own speed, rather than being handed one.
- **Stages Y136 through Y141 then took on exactly that challenge, and
  the outcome is a genuine turning point for this whole line of
  inquiry.** The first step confirmed the problem as precisely as
  possible: reading the actual code that decides speed shows there
  simply is no internal competition happening at all — the choice is
  made by a piece of external instruction, full stop, and nothing in
  the substrate's own rules ever gets a say. A careful search then
  checked whether the rules could produce that same distinguishing
  piece on their own if just given the chance, tracking every legal
  next step for many rounds and even exploring many alternate branches
  at once — and it never once appeared on its own. That looked like a
  dead end, but the next step found something genuinely encouraging
  hiding underneath it: the substrate's own structure, it turns out,
  already remembers which speed it's supposed to be at — a small,
  exact, reliable signature that's simply there, with nothing external
  needed to read it. The problem wasn't a missing memory. It was that
  nothing in the existing rules knew how to act on that memory — the
  closest thing available to a natural fix was found and tried, and it
  failed for an instructive reason: it would have let both speeds
  happen at once instead of choosing between them, and it was also
  fragile in a way that made it fall apart under its own reuse. That
  precise diagnosis pointed at exactly what was needed, and it was
  finally built: a new rule that looks only at the substrate's current
  shape — never an outside instruction — and picks the right speed
  every time, tested not just on the same objects it was built from
  but on an entirely new one it had never encountered before, and it
  still got every single case right. The last worry was whether this
  new rule could survive real competition with everything else the
  substrate might legally do at the same moment, rather than being
  called in a scripted order — and on its own, it couldn't: something
  else could beat it to the punch and break things half the time. The
  fix was to make the choice part of the very same step that moves the
  object, rather than a separate step that could be interrupted — and
  once that was done, it held up perfectly under genuine competition,
  stayed silent when nothing was moving, and never disturbed ordinary
  background activity at all. For the first time, this substrate has
  something that looks like it could actually decide its own motion,
  rather than simply being told what to do.
- **Stage Y142 then checked whether that new mechanism was a real
  discovery about the substrate or an accident of how its pieces
  happened to be numbered — and it passed a genuinely demanding
  version of that test.** Every step of the new rule was rebuilt to
  use only relationships between pieces — which piece came from which,
  which piece points which way — instead of any arithmetic on labels,
  and the result was then relabeled at random, thousands of times, and
  required to still pick the same answer. It did, essentially every
  time. As a sanity check on how meaningful that is, the old,
  label-dependent version of the same rule was put through the
  identical relabeling test and fell apart almost completely, getting
  the right answer only a handful of times out of thousands — which is
  exactly what should happen to something that was secretly relying on
  numbering, and confirms the new version's success wasn't a fluke of
  gentle testing. Along the way, this same careful checking noticed
  something nobody was looking for: a much older, more basic piece of
  the whole simulator — the general rule for how any local update
  orients itself — turns out to have the same labeling sensitivity
  problem, completely separate from anything about motion or speed.
- **Stage Y143 then fixed that more basic problem directly, and the
  result was reassuring in two places and genuinely significant in a
  third.** Every event making up the plain background material, and
  every single step of the object's own confirmed motion, came out
  completely unchanged under the fix — not one instance where the old
  and corrected versions disagreed, across the entire recorded history
  and every test performed on moving objects. But the object's
  resting, stationary condition — the one this entire investigation
  has built an enormous amount of work on top of since very early on —
  is different. The fix reveals that this resting condition actually
  has a handful of additional legal next moves available to it that
  were always technically there, just never taken, because the old
  labeling convention happened to hide them from view. This doesn't
  undo anything that's already been measured — every earlier result is
  still an accurate account of the specific path that was actually
  followed. What it opens is a real, previously invisible question:
  whether the resting state this whole program has treated as settled
  and stable actually is the unique outcome of the true underlying
  rules, or whether it's always had quiet extra doors that a
  numbering-blind description would have shown all along.
- **Stage Y144 then answered that question about as well as it could
  have gone.** Those extra doors turned out to come from one specific,
  identifiable choice in how the resting state's own internal tag was
  originally built — it had accidentally been given the same shape as
  an active, moving-sector signal, so a numbering-blind description
  correctly treated it as one. A single small correction to how that
  tag is written removes every one of the extra doors, changes nothing
  else about the resting state's behavior, and holds up under both
  random relabeling and, importantly, on species and situations it had
  never been built or tested against. The worrying possibility — that
  the resting state was never really stable to begin with — turned out
  not to be what happened. What remains open is narrower and more
  specific: the resting state's own internal clock, which decides when
  to move from one phase of its cycle to the next, is still being told
  what to do from outside rather than deciding for itself, exactly the
  same situation the moving object was in before it was fixed.
- **Stage Y145 then tried the same fix that worked for motion, applied
  to the resting state's internal clock — and got a result that's
  genuinely encouraging in one respect and a real reminder in
  another.** A reliable way to read which phase of its cycle the
  resting state is currently in, using nothing but its own present
  structure, was found and tested properly — trained on some objects
  and checked against entirely different ones it had never seen, with
  a perfect result both times. But knowing what phase something is in
  turns out not to be the same as making sure the right thing happens
  next, and that's exactly where this stage ran into trouble, in
  precisely the same way the moving case once did: right at the very
  first step of the cycle, there's a different, equally legal move
  available that would destroy the resting state outright if it
  happened to go first. On top of that, two more pieces of bookkeeping
  this whole mechanism still quietly depends on haven't yet been shown
  to arise naturally from the object's own structure. This isn't a
  step backward — a real, validated clock is a genuine result — but it
  suggests fixing the resting state's scheduling may turn out to take
  more than the one clean trick that worked for motion.
- **Stages Y146 through Y148 then closed all of it, completing a
  thirteen-stage architectural program that began by discovering the
  substrate couldn't choose its own speed at all.** The scheduling
  race that had just been found gave way to the same fix that worked
  for motion: folding the phase check and the next move into one
  single, indivisible step removed the rival possibility by
  construction, without needing to tell the substrate which choice to
  prefer. The harder remaining piece — how the resting state keeps
  track of which of its own pieces are due for cleanup, without an
  outside ledger — was solved with something genuinely elegant: a
  small, temporary marker that a piece of structure carries with it,
  pointing back to exactly what created it, built entirely out of
  ordinary relationships rather than any external bookkeeping. It was
  proven, not just tested, that this marker can never interfere with
  anything else going on — an actual logical proof that it's invisible
  to every possible legal move in the underlying rules, not just a
  large number of test cases that happened to come back clean. Every
  one of these pieces was then put together and run against the real,
  original simulator across the complete set of objects studied
  throughout this whole investigation, on objects and situations
  deliberately held back and never used to build any of it, under
  thousands of random relabelings, for hundreds of complete cycles —
  and every single test passed. For the first time, this substrate
  can determine its own speed, its own resting rhythm, and its own
  cleanup, entirely from its own current structure and history, with
  nothing handed to it from outside. That clears the specific
  architectural condition this investigation set for itself before
  daring to look at recurrence energy and inertia again.
- **Stage Y149 then took that first look, and the result is the most
  thoroughly earned confirmation of a disagreement this program has
  ever produced.** With every excuse removed — no external scheduler
  left to blame, no hidden bookkeeping, no non-covariant labeling —
  the same mismatch found many dozens of stages earlier came back
  completely unchanged. The quantity this investigation has treated as
  inertial mass since very early on still doesn't notice which kind of
  bond holds a composite together, while an independent measure of the
  same composite's structural content still does, by the same exact
  amount as before. Two objects with the identical bond count but
  different bond types have identical mass by the old measure and
  different content by the new one, and no single conversion factor
  can make those agree. That's not a new finding, but it is now
  essentially unimpeachable — the entire thirteen-stage architectural
  effort was, among other things, a very thorough test of whether this
  disagreement might quietly disappear once every possible scheduling
  or labeling excuse was removed, and it didn't.
- **Stages Y150 through Y152 then searched for something genuinely
  new instead of re-arguing the old disagreement, and found a result
  worth taking seriously even though it stopped short of a full
  answer.** The first attempt at measuring how much a composite's
  structure has to change during a change of speed failed its own
  basic requirement — it came out different depending on which
  direction the object was already facing, which a real physical
  resistance-to-motion shouldn't do — and that failure was reported
  honestly rather than patched. But a cleaner, more narrowly-defined
  version of the same idea, focused only on the connections between
  different pieces of a composite rather than everything happening
  near it, turned out to be remarkably well-behaved, and it was tested
  properly: the exact rule discovered on the original population was
  checked blind against eleven entirely different, more complex
  objects it had never been shown, and it held up perfectly. Only
  after that real test did anyone compare it to the old independent
  measures of structural content — and for the first time in this
  whole investigation, two completely separately built quantities
  agreed on the *direction* a topology difference should point, by an
  exact, fixed ratio, holding across every comparable case. This is
  not the same as showing they're measuring the same physical thing —
  the two quantities still don't agree on any single overall scale,
  and one of them isn't even defined for the simplest object in the
  population — but it's a genuinely new kind of agreement, earned
  rather than assumed. A follow-up attempt to turn this into something
  unambiguously positive succeeded at the arithmetic but ran into
  something more fundamental: the actual round-trip journey from
  resting to moving and back doesn't yet return every kind of
  composite to a state the system recognizes as the same resting
  condition it started from. That's a real, newly discovered gap in
  its own right, separate from everything fixed before, and the
  honest response was to stop and flag it rather than paper over it.
- **Stages Y153 through Y155 then closed that gap and delivered the
  strongest positive result this whole mass-energy program has ever
  produced.** The problem turned out not to be about bond type at all
  — it was about timing: trying to settle back into rest before the
  object had genuinely finished relaxing into its moving state could
  land it somewhere the system didn't recognize, for *any* kind of
  bond. Waiting for a real, structurally-defined sign that relaxation
  was actually complete, rather than just counting rounds, fixed this
  completely for the slower of the two speeds. The faster speed turned
  out to have a real, structural rule of its own: it can never return
  directly to rest at all — it always has to pass through the slower
  speed first, a genuine two-step gearbox rather than a shortcut.
  Interestingly, stopping this way lands on a second, different-looking
  resting condition that is nonetheless just as stable and self-
  sustaining as the original one — treated as two descriptions of the
  same kind of object's local surroundings, not as two different
  objects. With a real, fully closing round trip finally in hand, the
  transition-cost idea from before was rebuilt on it and passed a
  completely blind test on eleven more complex objects it had never
  been shown, with no exceptions anywhere. Only then was it compared
  against the earlier, independently-built measures of bond content —
  and for the first time in this entire investigation, one single
  reference comparison was enough to correctly predict the exact
  relationship for every other topology tested, including all eleven
  new objects, with nothing adjusted afterward. It agrees with one of
  those earlier measures and exactly disagrees, in a precise and
  predictable way, with the other — and this correction is reported
  openly as a reversal of an earlier, less complete version of the
  same idea, not quietly buried. What it still doesn't do is explain
  the single simplest object in the whole population, which has no
  bonds at all and therefore no value under this new measure, even
  though every other candidate assigns it one — so this remains a real
  agreement about how topology should be weighted, not yet a full
  answer to what the total quantity is.
- **Stages Y156 through Y159 then tried to fill in that missing piece
  and, in the process, found something more important than the piece
  itself.** The natural next step was to measure the single object's
  own version of the same cost and check whether adding it to the bond
  cost gives the right total for a composite — and the first attempt
  at this failed completely, but in an extremely well-understood way:
  because a composite's own internal structure keeps shifting which
  specific piece belongs to which constituent, simply asking "which
  piece is near me right now" quietly double-charges every time a
  piece changes hands without anything actually being built or
  destroyed. Fixing that by giving every piece a permanent, unchanging
  owner from the moment it's created helped, but exposed a second,
  subtler problem — some of what gets counted as "the object's own"
  material was actually just ordinary surrounding material that
  happened to wander into contact first. Fixing that too, by counting
  only material the object genuinely produced itself, led to the real
  discovery: for both kinds of bond, the amount of material genuinely
  produced by the bond itself, as opposed to just borrowed from the
  surroundings, comes out to exactly zero. Every single piece of
  structure sitting at a bond's connection point, the whole way
  through, traces back to ordinary background material, never to
  anything the bond made. That means the celebrated agreement from
  three stages earlier was never actually measuring how much structure
  a bond builds — it was measuring how much of the surrounding
  material gets reshuffled between "belongs to this constituent" and
  "belongs to the connection" as things move, which genuinely does
  differ between bond types without being anything the bond itself
  produces. The natural next question was whether that reshuffling
  material has any real influence over the object's actual motion, and
  the answer split cleanly in two: the specific kind of reshuffling
  behind the earlier result has no influence at all — deleting it
  outright changes nothing about which way the object goes next. But a
  different, separate piece of surrounding material was found that
  genuinely does change what the system currently believes its own
  speed to be, the first time in a very long stretch of this
  investigation that removing something from the environment has
  actually changed anything about the object's own state rather than
  simply being ignored. Whether that change ever turns into an actual
  difference in motion, or stays a relabeling with no consequence,
  is exactly where this leaves off.
- **Stage Y160 then answered that question, and the answer is the
  single most significant result the mass-inertia program has produced
  since it began.** Deleting nothing but that one piece of surrounding
  material, at exactly two moments, with no command about speed ever
  given, causes the object to genuinely speed up on its own — not
  briefly, not as an artifact of the intervention itself, but
  following a clean, repeatable, three-step progression that settles
  into the faster speed and then simply stays there under its own
  ordinary rules, with nothing further removed or added. That holds
  for every composite shape tested, every orientation, every context —
  no exceptions anywhere. The short delay before the speed actually
  changes turns out to have a completely ordinary explanation: each
  step the object takes is decided using the situation as it existed
  just before that step, so removing something can't reach back and
  change a step already taken — it only changes what's available for
  the next one, which is exactly why the effect shows up two steps
  later rather than immediately. Trying the same trick only once,
  instead of twice, didn't simply wear off the way a temporary nudge
  normally would — it settled the object into a different, in-between
  behavior that kept repeating itself indefinitely, which is reported
  honestly as an interesting, reproducible pattern worth investigating
  further rather than claimed outright as a third stable speed. This
  result stands on its own, separate from the ongoing question about
  bond content and topology — it doesn't retroactively prove the
  earlier bond measurements were secretly about resistance to motion,
  and the investigation is careful to say so. What it does establish,
  for the first time, is that this substrate's own future can actually
  be changed by altering its surroundings alone, with nothing handed
  to it from outside — which is the basic precondition for a real
  physical resistance to motion to even be a coherent question here.
- **Stages Y161 and Y162 then pushed on that new result from two
  directions at once, and both are worth knowing about for different
  reasons.** The odd, unplanned in-between behavior noticed briefly
  last time turned out to be completely real — left alone for a much
  longer stretch, the object settles into it immediately and just
  stays there, rocking back and forth in a stable, repeating pattern
  sitting cleanly between the two speeds already known, with nothing
  further done to it at all. That's a genuine third autonomous
  behavior, not a temporary in-between moment on the way to
  somewhere else. Trying to reverse the whole effect — talk the object
  back down from the faster speed to the slower one, again using
  nothing but removing and adding ordinary surrounding material — went
  well for one direction of motion and failed cleanly for the other,
  and the failure wasn't vague: at a specific, precise moment the
  proposed fix would have had to attach a new connection to a point
  that was already itself one of the two ends of that same connection,
  which the system correctly refused to allow. Rather than quietly
  tweak the rule and try again once that was seen, the exact geometric
  reason for the split was worked out and set aside for a fresh
  attempt. That fresh attempt, tried immediately after, failed too —
  but for an even more instructive reason: the rule that correctly
  describes the object once it has fully settled into its new speed
  turns out not to describe it correctly while it's still in the
  middle of settling, because the very things the rule depends on are
  themselves still shifting during that in-between stretch. A recipe
  that's exactly right for the finished dish doesn't necessarily work
  partway through cooking it.
- **Stage Y163 then solved it, and the answer turned out to be
  about restraint rather than precision.** Two careful searches for a
  better choice of where to attach the reverse-control connection both
  came back empty; the only candidate that matched every known success
  was the original one. The breakthrough came from changing something
  else: the old rule always added a new connection, even when a
  perfectly good one was already there, and in one direction of
  motion that duplicate caused the collision. Changing the rule to
  leave an existing connection alone, and only add one when nothing is
  present, made reverse control work in both directions, and even made
  several different attachment choices all work equally well. With
  that fixed, objects were driven back and forth between the two
  speeds repeatedly, four legs in a row with no resets, and it worked
  every time for every composite tested, including all 44 cases of the
  largest five-piece chains. The time each object took to fully settle
  after slowing down followed a simple rule written down before the
  data existed, and it was right in every case. All of this was
  carried out under difficult computing conditions with repeated
  timeouts and interruptions, and the checking software caught two
  real bookkeeping mistakes along the way, both disclosed and fixed
  without touching the scientific criteria. What it establishes is
  reliable, reversible, externally triggered two-speed control. It does
  not yet show that the substrate does this on its own, and it does not
  yet include rest or the in-between speed in one combined picture.
- **Stage Y164 finished by confirming that two particular three-piece
  composites, each held together by two bonds, keep exactly repeating
  themselves for 256 steps without any help.** (A later audit showed
  that the four "separate backgrounds" used here were in fact the same
  background: the vacuum turns out to be completely deterministic, so
  different random seeds produce an identical vacuum. The result still
  stands, but it is not the independent replication it was first
  described as.)
- **Stage Y165 was a pilot search for anything resembling mass or
  energy, and it took an instructive turn.** Tests on a single particle
  came back negative, and the reason is simple: a particle alone has
  nothing to push against or be pushed by, so there is nothing for an
  interaction to show up in. The work moved to pairs. Pairs initially
  fell apart, and careful reading showed why: at each step there are
  two legal moves that look almost alike, one that keeps the particle
  going and one, built from very freshly made material, that tears it
  apart. A single new scheduling rule was added, openly, as an
  extension of the model: when both are available, the move that
  continues an already established pattern goes first. With that rule,
  particles and ordinary background activity coexisted without any
  step being prescribed from outside, across every random seed
  tested. Then something interesting appeared. When two particles move
  in the same direction some distance apart, the one behind slows down
  from the faster speed to the slower one, reproducibly, in every
  random seed tested, and never when either particle is
  alone. Mirror the arrangement or reverse the direction of travel, and
  the one that slows switches exactly to whichever is now behind. The
  specific connection responsible can be traced back to the partner's
  region, and removing it in a check undoes the change every single
  time. This looks less like the two attracting each other and more
  like the follower running into the wake the leader left behind. It
  also echoes the earlier finding that adding a particular connection
  near a particle slows it down: here the partner appears to supply
  that connection naturally. Whether the effect comes from where the
  partner is now or from what it left behind is the next question.
- **The next Y165 step answered that question: it's what the partner
  left behind.** The leading particle was placed and set moving first,
  and the following particle was only added afterward. The follower
  still slowed down, every single time, and never when either particle
  was alone. The specific connections responsible had been laid down by
  the leader before the follower even existed, and by the time they
  mattered they sat several steps behind where the leader had moved to.
  Removing them undid the slowdown every time. So the leader leaves a
  lasting trail, and a follower that runs into that trail is affected
  by it. This is closer to a wake, or a stored memory in the
  background, than to the two particles acting on each other directly.
  One honest caution: the follower slowing means the gap between them
  grows, so if this is an interaction at all, so far it pushes them
  apart rather than together. How long the trail lasts, and whether it
  fades, is the next thing to measure.
- **A series of seven follow-up tests then pinned down what this
  interaction actually is.** The trail shows no sign of fading, even when
  the leader has moved a long way off. Newer activity by the leader,
  after the start, never reaches the follower at all. Two particles
  heading straight at each other do nothing to each other until they
  actually touch, which is what the investigator had predicted; a more
  detailed prediction offered in between, tying the onset to how far
  each particle's trail extends forward, was wrong. A simple
  Casimir-like picture, in which more activity on one side than the
  other pushes a particle, also failed. What the tests found instead is
  quite specific. Each particle sets its speed using one particular
  kind of connection. The partner's history affects that single slot,
  either by filling it directly or by making it possible for ordinary
  background activity to remove a connection the receiver was going to
  need. The effect also depends on what the receiver is already doing:
  a fast particle reliably slows, a slow one mostly doesn't change, and
  a particle on the in-between alternating rhythm can be knocked off it
  in either direction. One important caution remains. In every case,
  the responsible structure traces back to the very first moments of
  how the leading particle was placed into the simulation, not to
  anything it did afterward. That looks like the leftover traces of
  formation found much earlier in this investigation, and it raises the
  possibility that the effect depends on how particles are inserted.
  The next step is to check whether it survives when those traces are
  removed.
- **The next six tests followed that caution to its end, and the
  answer so far is sobering.** A clean bookkeeping rule for the
  interaction was confirmed, but nothing in it balances out, so it does
  not point to a conserved quantity like energy. The interaction also
  never cycles back; it just locks the receiver into a new speed. Then
  the search for the source. Removing the leftover formation trace did
  nothing. Placing a new receiver on the leader's recent path showed no
  effect at all, while placing it back at the original starting spot,
  long after the leader had moved far away, brought the effect straight
  back. The two worlds turned out to be identical around that spot out
  to seven steps; the first difference appears at eight. Removing the
  extra structure found there did nothing, and removing the structure
  missing there did nothing either. That left one explanation standing.
  The simulation chooses each next event by a single random draw across
  the whole world. Adding a particle anywhere changes what is available
  to choose from everywhere, so the background near the receiver
  unfolds differently even though nothing from the leader ever reaches
  it. In the tests so far, the two worlds being compared never had a
  single available event in common at the start. Whether the
  interaction is real or mostly a consequence of that shared random
  choice is now the central question, and the next test is designed to
  answer it directly.
- **That test (D3-7N) was more encouraging than expected.** Giving both
  worlds the exact same random choices, three different ways, made no
  difference to when the receiver first changed speed, counted in the
  receiver's own steps: the same step every time, in every case. The
  randomness shifts timing in the wider world, but not the moment the
  receiver responds. That pointed away from the start position and
  toward where the receiver is heading. Looking back at earlier runs, the
  first change happens exactly as the receiver arrives at the place
  where the leader was originally formed, in every positive case and
  nearly every negative one. That was noticed after the fact, so it
  proves nothing yet. The next test is built to settle it honestly: it
  will predict in advance exactly when, where, and in which of the two
  known ways the change should happen, then check that prediction with
  everything else in the world frozen so that only the receiver moves.
  If it works, the "interaction" is the receiver walking into what the
  leader's creation left behind, with nothing traveling to it. That would
  be a clean, local result, though it would still depend on how
  particles are placed into the simulation.
- **The prediction worked (D3-7O).** Before anything was run, a map of
  the exact spots where the receiver would look for the connection that
  sets its speed was used to predict three things: at which of its own
  steps it would change, exactly where it would be, and which of the two
  known ways the change would happen. The map even predicted that one
  of the eight cases would change two steps later than the others. With
  everything else in the world frozen so that only the receiver moved,
  all eight predictions came true exactly, and they came true exactly
  again with the world running normally. The picture is now clear: both
  particles travel the same way, the leader is long gone, and the
  receiver changes speed when it reaches the place where the leader was
  born, because something from that birth is still sitting there in the

  receiver earlier, found nothing, but a closer look showed the receiver
  simply never got far enough to reach that place in the time allowed,
  so that test needs to be rerun longer before it can say anything.
- **Next, the leader's birthplace was moved (D3-7P).** It was placed a
  little closer, at the usual distance, or a little farther away. Every
  prediction that could be properly qualified in advance was exactly
  right, in both the frozen and the normal runs. For one direction of
  motion the response moved with the birthplace every time: the
  receiver always changed speed right inside or at the edge of wherever
  the leader had been created. But the strict rule set before the test
  required every case to be predictable in advance with a known cause,
  and five of the 24 were not, so the law is officially not established.
  Two of those failures look like timing problems: when the birthplace
  was very close, the receiver probably reached it before the scored
  part of the run even began. Another surprise was that the kind of
  change, supplying the receiver's connection or taking one away,
  switched depending on the distance. The next step separates two
  questions that were tangled together: predicting when and where the
  receiver will respond, and tracing what created the difference it
  responds to.
- **The last two tests of this branch (D3-7Q and D3-7R) resolved it.**
  With the birthplace moved one unit at a time across nine positions,
  the prediction of when, where and how the receiver would respond was
  exactly right in all 72 normal runs. The switch between the two kinds
  of change turned out to repeat every three units, matching the rhythm
  of the receiver's own alternating motion. A longer early run showed
  the leftover is there from the very beginning, and removing or
  restoring the single connection involved erased the effect every
  time. Then the decisive check: the leader was prepared by two
  different, equally valid methods that end in exactly the same leader,
  identical in every measurable way. Most of the time the leftover was
  the same, but in a few close-range cases it differed. That means the
  effect depends on how the leader was made, not on the leader itself,
  so it is a memory of the preparation procedure rather than a genuine
  force between particles. It is a real, precisely understood local
  effect, but it can't answer the mass-energy question, and the program
  is returning to that question directly.
- **Back to mass and energy (Y165-C3).** Earlier pilot work had
  already shown that a single particle and the two-piece composites rest
  and repeat themselves exactly, but that there was no safe way to
  "touch" a resting particle to measure what it contains. C3 tried the
  gentlest possible touch the existing rules allow: letting one extra,
  otherwise legal move happen once, then leaving the particle alone. In
  every case where such a move existed, it used almost exactly the same
  pieces as the particle's own next step and wiped that step out, so the
  particle could no longer continue. An untouched copy carried on

  predictable: it's the same destructive competing move found earlier

  coupling would have to look like, starting with two environmental
  features already known to the program, and with a clear rule that
  anything added is reported as an extension of the model, not as a
  property of the original.

## 10A. Scaffolding audit: what was an artifact, how it was caught, and what remains

### Why this section exists

In this model, the only component that is genuinely "the physics" is the rewrite grammar. Everything else was built by the investigation to make experiments possible: how particles are inserted, how events are scheduled, how speed is classified, how bonds are seeded, how states are compared, and how quantities are counted. The substrate is highly sensitive to exact local structure, and a discrete system offers no averaging to wash out small choices. As a result, a design decision in the experimental apparatus can leave a fingerprint that looks like a physical effect.

This happened repeatedly. Many of the most promising moments in the program turned out to be properties of the scaffolding rather than of the substrate. This section records every such case identified so far, the method that exposed it, and how it was resolved. It then lists the scaffolding still in place, since each remaining item is a candidate source of future artifacts. The intent is that a reader can see exactly which results survived removal of the apparatus around them, and why those results should be trusted.

### How artifacts were caught

The same small toolkit exposed nearly every case:

1. **Matched controls** (solo, vacuum-only, receiver-only) run through identical schedules.
2. **Immutable provenance** from the lineage recorder built at Y96, which records which event produced every relation.
3. **Relabeling tests**, including negative controls that show an old rule failing under the same permutations.
4. **Surgical deletion and counterfactual arms** that remove one ingredient while holding everything else fixed (Y131 onward).
5. **Frozen protocols and sealed holdouts**, so criteria cannot drift after results are seen.
6. **Independent verifiers** that replay every scored event from frozen inputs.

### Scaffolding still in place

1. **Particle insertion.** Particles are placed by a prescribed procedure rather than formed from vacuum dynamics. This is the root scaffold; the open Y165 origin question sits directly on it. Native particle formation from the vacuum has not been achieved, and until it is, every multi-particle result carries some version of this caveat.
2. **The D3-4 scheduler rule**, which gives an established recurrence priority over a competing fresh-source gate. Multi-particle survival depends on it, and it redirects roughly one event in six.
3. **External speed-change triggers**, the environmental deletion and source insertion used in Y160–Y163.
4. **Prescribed preparation and warm-up rounds** preceding every autonomous scored phase.
5. **Observer definitions**, including the dressing quotient (CQ1), the speed classifier, and bond-seeding motifs.
6. **Vacuum preparation choices**, including finite vacuum sizes and the use of translated sites within one history as "contexts." The native vacuum is deterministic (one legal gate at every tick), so different RNG seeds do not produce different vacua; seed-to-seed variation arises only from scheduler choices after particles are inserted.
7. **The global sequential scheduler.** Each event is drawn by one random choice over every legal gate in the world. Because the rewrite rule is not causally invariant (event order changes outcomes, as the Y141 and Y145 races showed), this makes every region's history depend on events everywhere else. It is a non-local coupling channel built into the apparatus, and any claim about locality, light cones or particle interaction requires either a scheduler-matched control or a causally local update scheme.

### Standing rule adopted from this audit

Before any new positive result is promoted, it must pass at least one preregistered scaffold-removal control aimed at the most plausible apparatus-based explanation. The control must be designed so that it could succeed, and a result that survives it should be reported alongside the control, not in place of it.

A second preflight rule follows from the vacuum branching audit: before RNG seeds are treated as independent replicates, the branching factor of the relevant dynamics must be measured. If it is one, the seeds are not independent samples.
